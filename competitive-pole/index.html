The competitive equilibrium, and the ladder that reaches it.

The penultimate round of the contest has many equilibria. At one end sits tacit collusion; at the other, a single distribution under which both contestants earn nothing at all. The paper writes that far one down in closed form for integer power costs. This note explains what it is, shows the one equation it solves for any convex cost, shows exactly why the closed form exists only at integer powers, and works out the sense in which the ranked equilibria in between converge to it.

The equation. Imposed at every effort level it gives the competitive equilibrium; imposed at finitely many, it gives the ranked equilibria. That sentence is what this note unpacks.

    Note what this is not. The rival's effort does not reach you only through a probability of winning; it reaches you directly, through the lead you carry forward. Crossing a threshold here moves your payoff continuously — it is in the final round, not this one, that overtaking turns a loss into a win and makes the payoff jump. So this is not an all-pay auction, and no argument in this note turns on shading a rival by a hair or on a tie-breaking rule.

    2The kink, and why one game has many equilibria

    Fix the rival's effort and watch your own payoff as your effort rises. It has two regimes, divided by the junction where you stop being the marginal player and become the favourite.

    • Behind. While the lead you would carry forward stays below the junction, the continuation value is zero, so your payoff is just . Strictly concave, falling, best at zero: effort is pure waste.
    • Leading. Past the junction your payoff is . Also strictly concave — the first cost term has an argument that falls linearly in your effort, and a convex function of a linear argument, negated, is concave; the second is strictly concave outright.

    Two strictly concave pieces meeting continuously. But the left piece is falling as it arrives and the right piece climbs away, so the join is an upward kink and the payoff is not concave overall. A function like that has two local maxima: zero effort, and an interior peak out on the leading branch.

    This is the source of everything in this note

    Nothing else is needed. Which of the two peaks is higher depends on what the rival does, so as her effort varies the ranking of your two candidates flips; at the crossover you are indifferent between two efforts that are far apart, and indifference between distant efforts is what a mixed equilibrium is built out of. The same kink, sitting at a rival's atom instead of at the junction, is what forces the supports to interleave in section 4. One feature of the payoff, two consequences.

    Two polar equilibria, and a ladder between them

    What the kink produces is an equilibrium set with two poles and a ladder joining them.

    • At one end, collusive equilibria: one contestant inactive, the other taking the best payoff she can credibly hold. These are in pure strategies, and rent dissipation is minimal.
    • At the other, the competitive equilibrium: both contestants draw effort from a continuous distribution and both earn exactly zero. As in Bertrand competition, rent dissipation is total.
    • In between, the ranked equilibria: mixed, with finite support, the two supports never overlapping and always interleaving. The more effort levels in play, the lower the payoffs.

    3What the competitive equilibrium is

    Take the symmetric case: equal values and no lead, , so both contestants share one reach . This is where the competitive equilibrium lives, and not merely for convenience: at general convex cost an equilibrium in which both contestants randomize continuously requires the reaches to be aligned, and off the symmetric point they are not.

    We are after a pair of atomless distributions — no atom anywhere, so every single effort level has probability zero — under which both contestants earn nothing. The paper gives it in closed form for a power cost with an integer: the distribution function is a sum of exponentials,

    Three questions the closed form leaves open

    Why exponentials? Sums of exponentials are what solutions of linear constant-coefficient differential equations look like. Where does a differential equation come from here, and what happens when the cost is not a power?

    Why only integer powers? The proposition says integer, and that is not a convenience: section 7 shows the restriction is exactly right.

    In what sense is it a limit? The ranked equilibria of figure 2 march toward zero rent. Do their distributions actually converge to this one, and how fast?

    Sections 4 to 8 answer all three, and they answer them with a single object.

    Why they interleave — the kink again

    On any interval carrying none of her rival's atoms, a contestant's payoff is strictly concave, for exactly the reason section 2 gave. A strictly concave function has at most one maximizer; every effort she actually plays must be a maximizer; so at most one of her atoms fits in any such gap. What breaks the concavity, and so creates the gaps, is a rival atom. The value of overtaking one sitting at is

    (K)

    negative below it, exactly zero at it, positive and rising above it — overtaking by nothing is worth nothing. That is the kink of section 2 again, now sitting at each rival atom instead of at the junction.

    Interleaving follows in a line. Two atoms of the same contestant adjacent in the merged order would both sit inside one gap of the other's support, and a gap admits at most one. So the merged support alternates. It starts with contestant 2, whose bottom atom overtakes nothing and so is worth to her — which can only be an equilibrium payoff if it is zero. Her earning zero is a conclusion, not a labelling convention.

    The march: one number fixes the whole comb

    What just happened

    A finite sum over atoms became an integral against a distribution function, with no approximation and no limit taken. Equation (V) is an exact restatement of the equilibrium conditions of a comb with three teeth, or thirty. The discreteness has not been removed — it has moved, out of the equation and into the set of points at which the equation is required to hold. Keep hold of that sentence; section 8 is its consequence.

    Equation (V) is a Volterra equation of the second kind: the unknown appears once alone and once inside an integral whose upper limit is the point of evaluation. That last feature is what makes it friendly. Such an equation is really a forward recursion — the value at a point depends only on values strictly below it — so it can be built up from the left, the way one solves a difference equation forward in time. That answers the first of section 3's questions: the competitive equilibrium exists and is unique for every cost in the class, power or not. What is special about powers is not the equilibrium. It is the closed form.

    The relation between the two objects

    Now put the two facts from section 5 side by side.

    • The competitive equilibrium satisfies the Volterra identity (V) at every point.
    • A ranked equilibrium satisfies the same identity (V) at its own atoms only, with the leftover closure mass parked on the top atom, above every point at which the identity is checked.

    (M)

    Two equations at each tooth, for the two unknowns that tooth introduces: where the atom sits, and how much mass it carries. The competitive equilibrium satisfies both for free, because a function that vanishes everywhere vanishes at any node and has zero mean on any cell.

    The statement

    The march is the Volterra equation solved by marching. The whole algorithm is (ℛ) driven to a zero and a zero mean on each successive cell, and the ranked equilibria are exactly its solutions. Same equation as the competitive equilibrium, weaker demand: vanishing at finitely many points and on average in between, rather than everywhere. They are not approximations of the competitive equilibrium in any loose sense — each is an exact solution of an exactly posed finite problem.

    And this is where the word Volterra earns its keep. The kernel integrates over , not over the whole interval, so the residual at depends on the distribution only below . The two conditions at the k-th tooth therefore involve only atoms and masses that are already fixed. The system is triangular; the march is forward substitution on it, one atom-and-mass pair at a time, with the dial as the single constant a causal recursion needs in order to start. Replace the kernel by a Fredholm one, integrating over the whole interval, and every tooth couples to every other: there is no march at all, only a global solve. So the claim is stronger than the march can be written in terms of (V). The march exists because (V) is Volterra.

    It is worth being exact about which numerical scheme this is, because the obvious name is only half of it. Collocation imposes an equation at chosen nodes. The march imposes it at the nodes and imposes the cell means, and it needs that second condition precisely because the nodes are not chosen: they are unknowns, solved for jointly with the masses, so the mesh is an output of the scheme rather than an input to it. That is the one structural difference from every textbook version, and it is not a technicality. Every convergence theorem in that literature assumes a mesh you control, which is exactly what is unavailable here.

    What this does and does not buy

    The reading above is about the algorithm, and it is exact. On its own it says nothing about how close a given rung is to the limit: that is section 7's bound, which is conditional on a fine mesh, and whether the mesh does become fine along the ladder is proved at the quadratic and open in general. Turning this reading into a theorem about the limit would need the sign of the closure residual, which nothing here supplies. Importing a fact about the limiting object and asserting it of a finite rung proves nothing.
